Teacher guide

Teaching with Tiny Mind

Tiny Mind is a free browser lab where students raise, watch and take apart real language models: very small ones, built the same basic way as the chatbots they use. It teaches how these models learn from data, what is inside them, and how to test a claim about a model instead of trusting a pretty picture.

What students learn

How language models work

  • A model predicts the next piece of text, as a list of chances.
  • Training nudges its numbers toward the data it sees, so what it learns depends on that data.
  • Learning differs from memorizing: secret (held-out) stories tell them apart.

What is inside

  • Layers, attention heads and neurons in story models.
  • A router and experts in a mixture-of-experts model, the design behind many large AI systems.
  • Neurons in a small vision network that recognizes drawings.

How to test a claim

  • Highlights and attention maps are correlation; switching parts off is the test.
  • Make predictions before testing, change one thing at a time, and use controls.
  • A "busted" result is a finding. Several of Tiny Mind's own design studies came back negative.

AI literacy

  • Models are made from data and can be inspected; they are not magic.
  • Claims that a model "knows" or "thinks" something need evidence.
  • Bigger is not automatically clearer or better understood.

Before class

A day before

  • Open toontalk.github.io/tinymind on one class computer and visit every room you plan to use, so you know where things are.
  • On each student computer, open the rooms you will use once ahead of time. Each mind downloads the first time it is chosen and the browser keeps it.
  • If your network blocks Hugging Face, the 440 MB Giant Storyteller won't load. The 58 MB Big Storyteller works for every room that needs a story mind.
  • Print the student guide's notebook page, or have students copy its table.

Privacy

  • Nothing students make is uploaded. Saved work is a .zip file on their computer.
  • Work is also kept in the browser on that computer only, so shared computers may show another student's minds. "💾 save my work" is the reliable way to keep work.
  • With an AI key, the AI features send text (story requests, questions, clues) to the company that key belongs to, and nowhere else.

Lesson plans

Times are suggestions. Pairs work well: one student drives, the other keeps the lab notebook, and they swap halfway.

A. First look

one lesson · 50 minutes · no key needed

Question: how does a model decide what to write next, and how could we check?

  1. 5 min Warm-up: read the start of a story aloud and have the class guess the next word. Collect a few guesses and talk about why some are likelier.
  2. 10 min Watch a mind grow up, "a brand-new mind growing up": step from babble to whole sentences. Ask: what did it learn first?
  3. 20 min Look inside a mind with the Big Storyteller: type a story start, read the guess-o-meter, open one neuron card, then switch some heads off in the switch-off lab.
  4. 10 min Pairs share one guess they tested and whether it was busted or proved.
  5. 5 min Exit ticket: "What is the difference between a clue and a proof?"

B. Raise a mind, then hunt

three lessons · key optional
  1. Lesson 1, invent and write. In Teach a tiny mind, each pair invents a character with two special things and builds a 60-story storybook (50 to learn from, 10 kept secret). Without a key, students write stories or start from a ready-made storybook. Discuss why some stories are turned away: the mind knows only 512 pieces of words.
  2. Lesson 2, raise and watch. Press 🐣 start raising and watch the pink (secret stories) and blue (ordinary stories) lines. Ask for stories along the way. Then open the time machine's four-character recording and compare: when are the facts learned, and when does the mind start memorizing?
  3. Lesson 3, the hunt. In Hunt for a hidden fact, ask the mind about the character, check the answer is about that character, gather clues, and test them. Students report one busted clue and, if they find one, one proved clue.

C. Inside different minds

two or three lessons · no key needed
  1. Thought bubble: find the words a mind is "thinking ahead," then swap a thought and compare the three rewrites.
  2. Experts: work through the room's challenges, one expert switched off at a time. Discuss what "expert" turned out to mean.
  3. Tiny Eye: draw, inspect neurons, switch them off, watch the eye grow, and squeeze it. Close by comparing: what counts as a "part" in each of these minds, and how did you test what a part does?

D. Investigation project

two to four weeks

Students choose a question, write predictions before testing, collect evidence with numbers, and present a poster or short talk that includes at least one negative result. Starter questions:

  • Does a mind learn a character's facts faster with more replay of ordinary stories, or slower?
  • Which layer of the Big Storyteller matters most for finishing a sentence? How do you know?
  • Can you find a neuron whose two ends mean opposite things?
  • Which Tiny Eye neurons does it need to tell a cloud from a sheep?
  • In the Experts mind, how often does the word alone decide where it goes?

Room notes

For each room: the idea it teaches, what to listen for, a misconception to watch, and a question for discussion. Button names are in the student guide.

🐣

Teach a tiny mind

Nursery

The idea

Training on a small storybook changes what a model writes. Secret stories show whether it learned or memorized; the "alphabet" shows how little it can spell.

Listen for

"It's copying my stories now." "The pink line went back up." Those are signs of memorizing.

Watch for

"A lower line always means a better mind." In Tiny Mind's own tests, the step with the best story score had learned only about a quarter of its character facts; much more training was needed.

Ask

Why keep 10 stories secret? What would go wrong if the mind saw them?

⏪

Watch a mind grow up

Time machine

The idea

Learning happens in stages you can see: babble first, then words and sentences, then whole stories. In the character recordings, the specific facts come later still. No waiting, no mind needed.

Listen for

Students noticing which skills appear first, and when old skills get worse as new facts arrive.

Watch for

"Step 0 knows nothing." The character recordings start from a mind that already learned from millions of stories, which is why step 0 tells stories about Lily.

Ask

Why can learning new facts damage old storytelling? (The room's own question.)

🔬

Look inside a mind

Microscope

The idea

A guess forms layer by layer. Attention heads look back at earlier words; neurons respond to patterns, each with two ends.

Listen for

"The highlight says this head matters, so let's switch it off and see."

Watch for

"A neuron is one idea." Most respond to a mix, and in tests muting one neuron changed the story in fewer than half of cases, while turning one up nearly always did.

Ask

What evidence would convince you that a neuron "means" something?

💭

See what a mind is thinking

Thought bubble

The idea

A model can hold upcoming words before writing them. The J-lens makes those visible, and swapping one changes the story.

Listen for

Surprise that a mind "knows" an ending early, and careful comparison of the three rewrites.

Watch for

"It plans like a person." These are statistical traces, strongest in the Giant's middle layers. Small minds oversteer, so keep swap strength low.

Ask

Is "thinking ahead" the same as planning? What would you need to show?

👥

Meet a mind made of experts

Experts

The idea

A router sends each word to one of eight experts per layer, the design used by many large AI systems. Switching one expert off breaks something specific.

Listen for

Students predicting which expert a word goes to before checking, and switching off one expert at a time.

Watch for

"There's a math expert and an animals expert." Here, experts sort kinds of words (speech marks, "to," word endings), and the word itself mostly decides the route.

Ask

Why might a large company build a model this way?

👁

A mind that sees your drawings

Tiny Eye

The idea

A small vision network turns strokes into a guess among 24 kinds of things. Most of its last-stage neurons have a main job you can name.

Listen for

Students testing a neuron's job by switching it off, and trying drawings that fool it.

Watch for

"A bigger eye would be easier to understand." Tiny Mind's tests of eyes 40 times larger found better guesses but neurons no easier to name.

Ask

Why is it never 100% sure? (It was trained to keep a little doubt; the room explains.)

🎯

Hunt for a hidden fact

Hunt

The idea

To say where a fact is kept, you must switch parts off, and check with other names that the effect is about this character.

Listen for

Students using 🔬 is this really about this character? as a control before celebrating.

Watch for

"The fact is in one place." In the first Zog tests, no single head or neuron mattered; a whole layer's team did (55.6% fell to 22.8%).

Ask

If switching a part off changes every answer, not just this one, what have you found?

Assessment

The lab notebook is the main evidence of learning. A simple rubric:

SkillBeginningDevelopingSecure
Makes a testable claimDescribes what they sawStates a claim, but it can't be testedStates a claim that a switch-off or swap could bust
Predicts before testingNo predictionPredicts after seeing the resultWrites a specific prediction first
Records evidence"It changed"Notes the change in wordsRecords numbers (chances, steps) and what stayed the same
Uses controlsNoneChanges several things at onceChanges one thing at a time and checks other names or stories
Draws conclusionsOnly reports successesSays busted or provedExplains what a busted result shows about the mind

Exit tickets

  • What is the difference between a clue and a proof?
  • Why does a mind need secret stories?
  • Describe one guess you tested. Was it busted or proved, and how do you know?
  • An app says "this neuron is the cat neuron." What would you want to see?

Discussion beyond the app

  • If tiny minds are this hard to explain, what does that suggest about big ones?
  • Who should be able to look inside the AI systems people use every day?
  • Where did the stories these minds learned from come from, and does that matter?

Access and support

Kid mode
Tick kid in the guide panel for shorter, simpler tour lines; chances become "7 in 10" style odds.
Voice
Tick voice and the guide reads aloud. On the website, students can ask questions with the microphone in browsers that support speech, such as Chrome and Edge.
The tour
▶ in any room gives a spoken walk-through that points at each part.
Drawing
The Tiny Eye takes a mouse, a trackpad or a finger on a touch screen.
Extension
The Experts challenges and "draw something that isn't a dragon but makes it say dragon" stay hard even for strong students.

Troubleshooting

Slow or frozen
Use the Big Storyteller instead of the Giant, close other tabs, and keep to one big room at a time. Older computers do better with the smaller minds.
"Resting"
A room is waiting because another room is using the memory. Return to it or reload the page.
Old version showing
Reload fully with Ctrl+Shift+R (Windows) or Cmd+Shift+R (Mac).
Training is slow
Keep the Tiny Mind tab visible while a mind trains; some browsers slow hidden tabs a lot.
The guide won't answer
Answering typed questions needs an AI key (see Before class). The tour still works.
A download won't finish
School filters sometimes block large files or Hugging Face. Try the Big Storyteller, or download at home first on that computer.

Background for teachers

The minds

The story minds are Andrej Karpathy's llama2.c models (260 thousand, 15 million and 110 million connections) trained on TinyStories. The Expert Storyteller (19 million connections) and the Tiny Eye (83 thousand) were trained for Tiny Mind.

The stories

TinyStories (Eldan and Li) is about two million short stories written by AI models in words a young child knows. About 900 different words make up 90% of the text, which is why such small minds can write fluent stories at all.

The approach

Students invent their own character, so nothing about where it is stored can be looked up; it has to be found and tested. The app's language follows one rule: a highlight is a clue, and switching a part off is the proof.
